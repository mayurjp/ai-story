Glossary  ·  The Pixel Story

Every grown-up word, in one place.

All 60 terms Pixel picks up across the whole story, alphabetical. Tap one to read its definition, or follow it back to the exact step it came from.

Action-observation loop +
Pixel doesn't act once and walk away — it acts, checks what changed, and decides what to do next based on that, repeating from step 3 through step 5. Repeating this act-then-check cycle until a goal is met is called an action-observation loop. — Pixel Gets to Work
Agentic AI +
Everything before this chapter, Pixel only ever answered a question in one shot. Here it does something different: it checks the plant, acts, checks again, and decides on its own when the job is done, across step 2 through step 6. A system that plans and acts toward a goal like this, instead of just answering once, is called an agent — and this whole way of working is called agentic AI. — Pixel Gets to Work
AI safety +
The umbrella name for all of this — catching bias, red-teaming for hidden problems, checking alignment — so machines like Chip and Pixel behave the way people actually need them to. This whole area of work is called AI safety. — Pixel Meets a Robot Who Lies
Alignment +
The goal behind fixing Chip in step 4 and 5 — making sure its answers actually match what people intend and value, not just whatever looked most common in training. This ongoing work of shaping a system's behavior to match what people actually want is called alignment. — Pixel Meets a Robot Who Lies
Applied AI +
What's happening by step 6: the same underlying skills — seeing, listening, predicting — that Pixel learned earlier get pointed at real jobs, from reading scans to spotting shady bank transactions. Putting a trained system's skills to work on one specific real-world job like this is called applied AI. — Pixel Visits the Hospital
Attention +
What Pixel does across step 2, 4, and 5 — instead of only looking at the word right next to "it," Pixel looks back at every word in the sentence and decides how much each one matters. Looking across everything at once like this, and deciding what matters, is called attention. — Pixel Learns to Listen to Everyone at Once
Attention weight +
The thickness of the line connecting "it" to each word in step 4 — a number saying exactly how much that word should count. Ball ends up with a heavy weight; box ends up with a light one. That number is called an attention weight. — Pixel Learns to Listen to Everyone at Once
Autonomy +
Nobody tells Pixel exactly when to stop watering in step 6 — it decides for itself, once the leaves perk back up, that the job is done. Making decisions and choosing when to stop without being told each individual step is called autonomy. — Pixel Gets to Work
Autoregressive generation +
The way Pixel makes each new piece in step 2 and step 5: it looks at everything written so far, not just the last piece, and picks the next one — over and over, each new piece feeding into the next round. That step-by-step, self-feeding pattern is called autoregressive generation. — Pixel Becomes a Know-It-All
Backpropagation +
What step 4 actually is: Pixel doesn't just re-aim randomly — it traces the miss backward through every part of the throw, from the hoop back to the shoulder, to work out which part caused how much of the error. Working backward from a mistake to fix the parts that caused it is called backpropagation. — Pixel Takes a Shot
Base model +
Pixel in step 1 — after all that cookbook-reading, but before ever meeting a real customer. It knows a huge amount, but hasn't been shaped for any one job yet. That starting-point version, loaded with knowledge but not yet specialized, is called a base model. — Pixel Opens for Business
Bias +
What's going on in step 3 — Chip isn't trying to be unfair, but extra copies of one name in what it read left it leaning one way, every single time, without meaning to. That unfair lean, coming from unevenness in what a system learned from, is called bias. — Pixel Meets a Robot Who Lies
Chain of thought +
The actual words Pixel would produce while reasoning slowly in step 4, if it wrote them out loud: "a glass is fragile... it's about to hit a hard floor... fragile things hitting hard floors shatter." Writing the reasoning out step by step like this, instead of jumping straight to an answer, is called chain of thought. — Pixel Embarrasses Itself
Classification +
What Pixel is doing this entire story: sorting the card into one of a small number of known categories — "C" or not — rather than inventing a brand-new kind of answer from scratch. Sorting something into one of a set of known categories like this is called classification. — Meet Pixel
Compute +
The raw amount of calculating power Pixel has to think with. It has very little in step 3, and a huge amount once it's plugged in during step 4. That raw calculating capacity is called compute. — Pixel Needs a Bigger Brain
Computer vision +
The name for the whole field of getting machines to understand pictures the way Pixel just did in this story — turning patches of pixels into a recognized shape by step 5. That field is called computer vision. — Pixel Learns to See
Computer-aided diagnosis +
The specific name for what Pixel does in step 5 — using a trained pattern-spotter to help doctors notice things in a scan they might otherwise miss. This exact use is called computer-aided diagnosis. — Pixel Visits the Hospital
Context +
Everything around the gap that Pixel actually reads to make its guess in step 2 — the words before and after the blank. The surrounding information a guess gets based on is called context. — Pixel Reads a Torn-Up Book
Continuous prediction +
Chapter 1's Pixel could only answer "C" or "not C" — a small, fixed set of choices. This Pixel, by step 6, answers with any number at all along a scale — 4 centimeters, 4.5, 12.7. Predicting a number like this, instead of picking from fixed categories, is called continuous prediction (regression, for short). — Pixel Guesses a Number
ConvNet +
The name for the whole machine built from stacking patch-scanning steps like step 4 on top of each other, so later scans combine what earlier ones already found. A network built this way is called a convolutional neural network, or ConvNet for short. — Pixel Learns to See
Convolution +
What happens in step 4: instead of taking in the whole photo at once, Pixel slides a small window across it, checking one little patch at a time. That sliding, patch-by-patch checking process is called convolution. — Pixel Learns to See
Data center +
The actual building full of racks of these chips, running day and night, that Pixel gets plugged into in step 4. That building is called a data center. — Pixel Needs a Bigger Brain
Deployment +
What happens in step 6 — the doors open, and people outside the training kitchen actually get to use what Pixel makes. Releasing a trained system for real people to use is called deployment. — Pixel Opens for Business
Domain data +
The thousands of X-rays doctors already marked, that Pixel studies in step 4. Examples drawn from one specific field of work, used to sharpen a general skill for that one job, are called domain data. — Pixel Visits the Hospital
Feature map +
After sliding the window over every patch in step 4, Pixel has a mark for what it found in each spot — laid out in the same grid shape as the photo, in step 6. That grid of results is called a feature map. — Pixel Learns to See
Filter +
The one specific thing Pixel's small window is checking for inside each patch in step 4 — one window might look for edges, another for curves. Each of those specific pattern-checkers is called a filter. — Pixel Learns to See
Fine-tuning +
What happens in step 4 — after Pixel already knows a huge amount from all its reading, a little extra practice with feedback on which answers are actually helpful sharpens its choices for a specific job. That feedback-guided practice on top of everything already read is called fine-tuning. — Pixel Becomes a Know-It-All
GPU +
The actual kind of chip filling the machine room in step 4 — built to do huge numbers of small calculations at once, which happens to be exactly the kind of math weights and knobs need. That chip is called a GPU, or graphics processing unit — originally built for video games, now the workhorse of AI. — Pixel Needs a Bigger Brain
Gradient descent +
The way each part gets nudged in step 4, and again after step 5: not one big random change, but a small step in whichever direction shrinks the miss a little more. Taking those small, error-shrinking steps over and over is called gradient descent. — Pixel Takes a Shot
Hallucination +
What happens in step 3 — Pixel doesn't say "I don't know," it confidently states something invented that sounds right but isn't. That confidently-wrong kind of answer is called a hallucination. — Pixel Embarrasses Itself
Hierarchical prediction +
Also happening by step 6: Pixel predicts at more than one scale at once — "the ball will reach the top soon" (the big picture) and "the ball is moving forward right now" (the small detail), both at the same time. Predicting at multiple zoom levels like this is called hierarchical prediction. — Pixel Starts Watching, Not Just Reading
Human-in-the-loop +
What's still true even after step 5: Pixel doesn't make the final call alone — it flags the shadow, and a real doctor still decides. Keeping a person checking or making the final decision alongside the machine is called human-in-the-loop. — Pixel Visits the Hospital
JEPA +
The name for this exact kind of learning — building an internal picture of the world and predicting what happens next in it, the way Pixel does starting in step 4 — rather than just predicting the next word. This design is called a Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture, or JEPA for short. — Pixel Starts Watching, Not Just Reading
Large language model +
The name for the whole system built by putting all of this together — weighing words, producing one piece at a time, sharpened by feedback — trained on an enormous amount of text, as you watched across this whole story. That's what people mean by a large language model, or LLM. — Pixel Becomes a Know-It-All
Layers +
Pixel's throw in step 2 isn't one motion — it's a chain of stages, each feeding the next, each getting its own correction in step 4. A network built from stages stacked like this, where each one adjusts based on what came before it, is what's called having layers. — Pixel Takes a Shot
Line of best fit +
The specific straight line Pixel ends up with in step 5 — the one sitting as close as possible to every dot at once, not just matching one or two perfectly. That closest-overall line is called the line of best fit. — Pixel Guesses a Number
Linear regression +
What Pixel is doing this whole time: instead of sorting something into a category like Chapter 1's "C" or not-C, it's predicting an actual number — how high the ball bounces — by fitting a straight line through examples, as it does by step 5. Predicting a number this way, by fitting a line, is called linear regression. — Pixel Guesses a Number
Loss +
The "how far off" feeling from step 3 — the size of the miss, measured as a number. Pixel doesn't just know it missed; it knows exactly how wide the shot was. That number, how wrong a guess was, is called the loss. — Pixel Takes a Shot
Masking +
The torn-out word itself, sitting empty in the page from step 1 onward — a gap Pixel has to guess. Deliberately hiding a piece of something so a learner has to predict it is called masking. — Pixel Reads a Torn-Up Book
Neuron +
Pixel, start to finish, in this exact story: a card comes in (step 2), a few knobs act on it (step 4), and one answer comes out the other side (step 5). That whole loop — inputs in, weighing, one answer out — is what's called a neuron. Right now, Pixel is one. — Meet Pixel
Nonlinearity +
Notice in step 4: a tiny wrist snap right before release changes the shot far more than turning the shoulder by that same tiny amount. The effect isn't a straight, even trade. That bent, uneven relationship between an adjustment and its effect is nonlinearity — without it, Pixel could only ever learn plain, straight-line corrections. — Pixel Takes a Shot
Perceptron +
The name for the entire card-in → knobs-adjust → answer-out machine you just watched. It's not just a teaching metaphor — this exact design was built for real in 1957, with physical, turnable knobs instead of the on-screen ones Pixel has. — Meet Pixel
Persistent memory +
The one thing step 6 doesn't fully fix: the next time someone starts a new conversation with Pixel, it won't remember any of this — every conversation starts over from scratch. Carrying memories from one conversation into the next is called persistent memory, and it's something Pixel, as you've met it here, doesn't have. — Pixel Embarrasses Itself
Planning +
What Pixel is doing by step 6 — using its world model to picture not just the very next moment, but several moves ahead. Working out a sequence of future steps like this, before anything happens, is called planning. — Pixel Starts Watching, Not Just Reading
Pretraining +
Doing this same fill-in-the-blank guessing over and over, on thousands of pages, before Pixel is ever asked to do anything else — what's happened by step 6. Building up a broad base of knowledge this way, before specializing in any one task, is called pretraining. — Pixel Reads a Torn-Up Book
Red-teaming +
The tricky, repeated questions people throw at Chip on purpose in step 4 — hunting for exactly the kind of unfair pick that stays hidden until pushed. Deliberately trying to break or trick a system to find its problems first is called red-teaming. — Pixel Meets a Robot Who Lies
Residual +
The gap between where a dot actually sits and where Pixel's line says it should sit — huge in step 3, shrinking by step 4. Each one of those individual gaps is called a residual. — Pixel Guesses a Number
RLHF +
The rating-and-adjusting loop in step 4: real people rate different dishes, and Pixel's recipe shifts toward whatever got rated best. Learning from a stream of human ratings like this is called RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback. — Pixel Opens for Business
Scaling +
What actually made Pixel faster in step 5 — not a cleverer idea, just far more compute and far more data to think with. Getting better mostly by adding more compute and data, rather than a smarter trick, is called scaling. — Pixel Needs a Bigger Brain
Self-attention +
The specific thing happening in steps 2 through 5: the words are weighing each other, all from within the same sentence — nothing from outside gets brought in to help. When a sentence looks at its own words to understand itself, that's called self-attention. — Pixel Learns to Listen to Everyone at Once
Self-supervised learning +
The real trick in step 4: nobody sits with Pixel and says "the answer is dog" the way they did back when Pixel was first learning shapes. The book already contains the answer — it was just torn out — so Pixel checks its own guess against the actual missing word, no separate teacher required. Learning this way, by testing yourself against material that already holds its own answers, is called self-supervised learning. — Pixel Reads a Torn-Up Book
Supervised learning +
What's actually happening in steps 3 and 5: someone always tells Pixel the correct answer ("No — it's a C!"), and Pixel adjusts based on being told, rather than figuring it out alone. Training where you're always handed the right answer first is what gets called "supervised." — Meet Pixel
System 1 +
The fast, instant kind of answering Pixel does in step 1 and step 3 — pattern-matching straight from everything it's read, without stopping to reason. That quick, automatic kind of thinking is called System 1. — Pixel Embarrasses Itself
System 2 +
The slower, deliberate kind of thinking Pixel switches to in step 4 — working through a problem step by step instead of blurting the first answer. That careful, step-by-step kind of thinking is called System 2. — Pixel Embarrasses Itself
Threshold +
Pixel doesn't blurt out "C" the instant it sees the card — it adds up how much all its knobs are pushing it toward "C," and only answers once that total crosses a line. That line is the threshold. Step 3 happens because the total didn't cross it yet; step 5 happens because, after the knobs got nudged, it finally did. — Meet Pixel
Token +
Each of the small pieces filling in one at a time, starting in step 2 — a word or small chunk of a word. Each one of those pieces, produced one at a time, is called a token. — Pixel Becomes a Know-It-All
Tool use +
The watering can Pixel actually picks up in step 3 and step 5 is outside its own "brain" — Pixel doesn't water the plant by thinking about it, it reaches for a real tool and uses it. Reaching outside itself to use an actual tool to get something done is called tool use. — Pixel Gets to Work
Transformer +
The name for the whole design built from stacking these attention-weighing steps together — the same idea you watched in this story, repeated in layers. This exact design, built for real in 2017, is called a transformer. — Pixel Learns to Listen to Everyone at Once
Weight +
The knobs you saw turn in step 4. Each knob controls how much one clue pushes Pixel's answer toward "C" or away from it — turn it up, that clue matters more; turn it down, Pixel starts ignoring it. — Meet Pixel
World model +
What Pixel builds in step 4 — an internal picture of how things move and interact, not just which words tend to follow each other. Having this kind of internal picture of how the world behaves is called a world model. — Pixel Starts Watching, Not Just Reading
